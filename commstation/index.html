CommStation

A touchscreen station for the truck, built on a Raspberry Pi. Map, GPS, mesh messaging, weather, radio and an emergency card on one screen, and it keeps working when the cell signal is gone.

Watch the CommStation video

What it is

The NomadCore app puts your family plan on your phone. CommStation is the same idea for the vehicle: a 7-inch touchscreen on the dash that knows where you are, talks to your family's radios, and holds the reference library, with no internet needed once it's set up.

It runs on a Raspberry Pi 5 with a battery backup that rides through engine starts. A small USB GPS or your phone gives it your position. Plug in a Meshtastic radio and you can text other family radios with no cell towers. Plug in a software radio and it listens for weather and digital radio traffic. Everything else (maps, places, directions, first-aid steps) is stored on the Pi's 256 GB memory card.

It's our own build, running in a Texas pickup today. We're sharing the parts list so you can see what it takes. It isn't a product you can buy, and the software isn't published yet.

What it does

Offline works with no signal. Online needs internet and falls back to the saved copy without it.

Offline map Offline

Vector map stored on the Pi, day and night themes, terrain shading, contour lines and public land (BLM, Forest Service, parks) for finding a camp.

Your position Offline

From your phone over the truck's own Wi-Fi, or a USB GPS. Speed, heading, altitude, and the clock set from GPS time.

Mesh messaging Offline

With a Meshtastic radio: text family radios with no cell service, see them on the map with distance and direction, and send an SOS beacon that reports whether it was delivered.

SOS card Offline

One tap from the map: your coordinates and what to say, radio steps for a call for help, and a printable copy with the family evacuation plan.

Find nearest Offline

About a million places across North America: fuel, hospitals, pharmacies, water, camping, police, vets, defibrillators. Sorted by distance and direction.

Directions Online Offline

Turn-by-turn with live traffic and faster-route offers when online. With no signal it still finds addresses and plans routes on the Pi, just without traffic.

Weather Online

Forecast and National Weather Service alerts for where you are, home, and where you're headed. Offline it shows the last saved forecast and how old it is. Sunrise, sunset and moon are always worked out on the Pi.

SDR and scanner Offline

A software-defined radio, installed and running: a scanner, NOAA weather radio, APRS position reports and digital modes (FT8, JS8, CW, SSTV) in the dashboard. The handheld works as a second receiver. All receive-only.

Library Offline

The NomadCore procedure library (9,333 procedures) and offline medical references, searched on the Pi. Every result shows its source and page.

Ask the AI Offline

Ask a question in plain words. AI models running on the Pi itself point you to the right procedure or write an answer from the library, with no internet. Repeat questions come back at once from memory.

Travel log Offline

Trips record on their own: route, stops, miles, time and weather along the way. Save any trip as GPX.

Supplies Offline

Your household inventory from the same CSV the NomadCore app imports, with what's expired, expiring, or running low.

Device status Offline

A status bar shows at a glance which GPS, mesh radio and other devices are connected and working.

Wi-Fi scanner Offline

An ESP32 board lists every Wi-Fi network around the truck, tags open and known ones, and logs each on the map where it was strongest.

Phone link Bluetooth

Pair your phone and make calls from the screen: contacts, recents and a keypad, an incoming-call banner, and call audio through the cab speakerphone. Incoming texts show in a big box and are read aloud while the truck is moving, with spoken replies. Contacts stay on the Pi. Calls still go over your phone's cell signal.

The screen also locks with a PIN or passphrase.

Ask: AI that runs on the Pi

Ask runs two AI models on the station itself, so it answers with no signal. Unless you use the optional web search, your questions stay on the Pi.

Laya, the router

A small model we trained on our own set of emergency and travel questions. In about five seconds it decides where a question belongs: a procedure in the library, or a written answer. If the library has a procedure, you get the library's exact steps rather than a paraphrase, and questions that need professional care get a red Call 911 banner.

Granite, the writer

IBM's open Granite 4 model (3B) running in Ollama. For everything else it writes an answer from library procedures and offline references, and lists the sources it used. When the truck has internet it can add a web search as one more source; that sends the question to the search service. It can also summarize a long procedure on request.

Memory and caching

  • Answer memory. Every finished answer is saved on the Pi. Ask the same thing again, even with the words in a different order, and the saved answer shows at once, without the minute the Pi takes to read sources. It says it's from memory and when it was made, and Look it up again gets a fresh one.
  • Memories expire. After 30 days, or 12 hours for questions about right now, such as the weather.
  • Earlier answers as sources. A new question on a topic it has answered before can use that earlier answer as one extra source, marked not checked. The model is told to trust the library, references and web over it. This is never done for medical questions, so a mistake can't be passed along.
  • Conversations. Follow-up questions keep the last few exchanges as context.

AI can be wrong. For first aid, the library's procedures are shown as written, and the station tells you when to call 911. It isn't a substitute for professional medical advice.

Coming next

Drone link

The station's side is built: it flies one ArduPilot drone over a 915 MHz telemetry radio, with no internet. The drone shows on the map with its height, battery and trail, and five big buttons cover it: Take off, Go here, Hold, Return home and Land. It stays within 400 ft up and 400 m of home, so it stays in sight. So far it has flown only in a simulator. The drone is its own build, about $650–1,100 on top of the station (frame, flight controller, GPS, telemetry radios, hand controller, Remote ID, batteries), and needs FAA registration.

Home security cameras

Check on the house from the truck: your home security cameras on the station's screen. This is planned and not built yet.

Wi-Fi scanner and logging

Wi-Fi scanner

A small ESP32-S2 Wi-Fi board rides on the Flipper Zero's header and reports what it hears to the station over the truck's own Wi-Fi, so it takes no USB port.

  • Every Wi-Fi network in range, strongest first, tagged OPEN (no password) or SAVED (one the station knows).
  • An alert when a saved network comes into range.
  • How busy each 2.4 GHz channel is, and the quietest one for the truck's network.
  • It only listens. The only network it joins is the truck's own.

Logging

  • Every network heard is logged on the Pi at the spot where it was strongest, and Show on map draws them: saved, open and the rest in their own colors.
  • The station also keeps its own records on the Pi: trips and stops, mesh messages, SOS beacons and whether each was delivered, and every drone command.
  • The logs stay on the Pi. Nothing is uploaded.

What else it costs

  • Data: the maps, places and medical references are free, open downloads (OpenStreetMap, Protomaps, USGS, Kiwix). The first download is large, tens of gigabytes, so do it on home Wi-Fi.
  • Online extras: live traffic and gas prices use TomTom and Google free tiers with their own keys. The station caps its own use under those free limits. Skip them and it still routes offline.
  • Licenses: see below. A GMRS license is a one-time FCC fee that covers your immediate family for 10 years.
  • No subscription. Nothing here is paid monthly.

Radio rules (US)

Listening is open to everyone. Transmitting has rules.

  • Meshtastic on 915 MHz needs no license. Leave the radio's "licensed" (ham) mode off.
  • NOAA weather radio is receive only.
  • GMRS needs an FCC license and a radio certified for GMRS. The UV-5R is not certified for GMRS, so use a certified GMRS radio for everyday family talk.
  • Amateur (ham) bands need at least a Technician license to transmit.
  • In the station, the software radio and the Digirig are set up to receive only.

This is a summary, not legal advice. Check current FCC rules before you transmit.
